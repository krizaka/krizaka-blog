This week I understood that my agents were not short of speed. They were short of a platform. Every session finished its work, opened a pull request, then waited for CI to pass, for the merge queue to accept it, for the branch to be up to date. While it waited, the session kept costing, and the pile kept growing.

This chapter tells the small system that got things moving again. I call it the train, because that is exactly what it is: wagons leaving one behind the other, never overtaking.

## The problem: a pile

We work with five lanes: one session per application (Yomizaka, Akinaza, Orazaka), one for the platform, one for security. Each opens its PRs. Thursday evening there were about a hundred open PRs across the organisation. At 2 a.m. on Friday there were 140, and an hour later Akinaza's repository carried 24 of them alone.

The bottleneck was not the code. It was the capacity of CI and of the merge queue: one PR passes, another waits behind it, and if the branch moves in between, everything starts over. A session that waits is a session burning budget to watch a circle turn.

## The train

The train is a small script: under eighty lines of shell, with no model token at all. It takes PRs that have already been read, in order, and for each one:

<Steps>
  <Step title="Wait">The previous PR is merged. Not before.</Step>
  <Step title="Update">The branch catches up with `main`. If GitHub changed its base, it follows.</Step>
  <Step title="Approve">Once per version of the branch, with my account.</Step>
  <Step title="Enqueue">The PR enters the merge queue.</Step>
  <Step title="Stop at the first red">A red check, a conflict, an ejection: the train stops and says why. It never merges around a failure.</Step>
</Steps>

The important point: the review happens before, by the session that wrote the PR, not by the train. The train judges nothing. It does the platform work, the kind an agent does badly because waiting for it is expensive.

  <Stage title="Branch" note="one session">A worktree from main, one issue, the test first. The session reads its own diff before it opens the PR.</Stage>
  <Stage title="Checks" note="CI on the PR">Lint, tests, build, code analysis. A red check stops everything here.</Stage>
  <Stage title="The train" note="no model token" tone="gate">It waits for the previous PR, updates the branch, approves once per version, enqueues. It stops at the first red.</Stage>
  <Stage title="Merge queue" note="CI again">The checks run once more, on the PR merged with what is ahead of it in the queue.</Stage>
  <Stage title="main" note="one commit">The PR becomes one commit on main, and its branch is deleted.</Stage>

The train did not work the first time. Each failure became a rule, and each rule has its test.

## The rules that came out of it

The train also changed our working rules, which matter more than the script:

- **At most 5 open PRs per repository.** At the limit, nobody opens a new one: we merge first.
- **Finish before starting.** A session's first job is its open PRs, oldest first: land them, or close them saying why.
- **One bulk PR per repository at a time.** A new organisation rule joins the open bulk PR instead of opening another.
- **A landing order.** Two PRs are stacked only when the first is already in the queue.

Rules that live in a document get forgotten, so each one became a guard that refuses, with its test:

A pile of open PRs tells me one thing: the order of the work was wrong. The train relieves the symptom, the rule of five treats the cause.

## What it changes

The figures I can give are the ones I can read back. I counted them from the GitHub API: for each hour, every pull request of the organisation that was created before it and not yet closed. Akinaza's repository went from 24 open PRs at 3 a.m. on Friday to 4 by the evening. Across the organisation, the pile went from 140 to 50 as I write, and 317 PRs were merged on Friday.

The peak at night is not a mystery: that is when the sessions finish their work and open their PRs, while the trains still wait for CI. The fall in the morning is the trains, landing one PR after the other.

I give no "time saved": I did not measure it, and I would rather stay quiet than invent a number. What I see is that sessions no longer spend their time waiting, and that we stopped stepping on each other.

## What is left

The train depends on CI, and CI is still slow. The ceiling of 5 PRs per repository is a rule that a human or an agent can forget: we still check it by hand at each checkpoint. And when the API quota is empty, everything stops, which has already happened once.

## My motto, and economy mode

This week I also put one sentence at the head of all our agents' rules: "The flow keeps running, the deadline is ours: it may move, the quality never does." The flow is the trains and the small PRs. The deadline is ours to set, and we say when it moves. The quality is not trimmed to hold a date: no skipped hook, no weakened test, no "good enough" render.

When my Claude usage reached 75 % of the week on Friday, we went into economy mode: two sessions at most, a cheaper model for repository chores, no paid renders. The trains kept working without spending a single model token. Economy mode lasted a few hours, then I decided to carry on. That was my choice, and my budget.

## Frequently asked questions

### Does a train replace review?

No. Review happens before, by the session that writes the PR. The train does not judge the code: it waits, updates, approves and enqueues.

### Why not GitHub's auto-merge?

We use it too. The train covers what it does not: waiting for the previous PR, bringing the branch up to date after a base change, and stopping at the first problem while saying so.

### Why 5 PRs per repository?

Because 24 open PRs on one repository means 24 branches rebasing on each other. Five is what we manage to land in a day.
