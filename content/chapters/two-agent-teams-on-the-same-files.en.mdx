A few days ago, we posted on LinkedIn the readable log of an agent team at work. The picture carried a plain note: it is a simulation, we show the idea, not our code. This time it is not a simulation. It is the same console, but it replays the log of a real run, on 19 November, on Verikora, our product whose code is public. Anyone can open and check every pull request I name here.

The question is simple. When several agent teams work at the same time on the same product, what happens when two of them touch the same file without knowing it? Among people, it is stepping on each other's toes: two changes that collide, a conflict to untangle, work to redo. We wanted to see, on a real and measured case, whether our way of working avoids it.

## The idea, in broad strokes

It stays a technology of our own, but the idea fits in four sentences.

Several agent teams work at the same time, each on its own ticket, without talking to each other. Before a team opens its pull request, a gate compares its files with those of the pull requests already open: if two teams touch the same thing, it sees it before they collide. Then a train lands the pull requests on the main branch, one at a time, each on the latest version. And when a pull request rests on another (it is stacked), the train waits for the first to land, then replays the second on top.

Nobody tells the teams to avoid each other. The point is to see whether the system is enough.

## 19 November, as the log wrote it

We launched two agent teams, separately, on two real Verikora tickets. Team A was fixing a label: a blocking finding in a file must read “À corriger” (to fix) everywhere, in the header, the library and the home. Team B was fixing an upload: a 24-megapixel phone photo failed with no reason. Two unrelated subjects, but both touched the site's file that describes documents, and the four files of the interface's words. Nobody had planned it.

## S1: two teams, the same files

At 16:38, team A opens its pull request, [#889](repo:verikora/pull/889). The train takes it at the head.

At 16:44, team B is about to open its own. Before it exists, the gate compares: B's branch shares 5 files with #889. It notes it in the log. As both are fixes, it does not hold B back: a fix goes first, that is our rule. Two seconds later, B opens [#890](repo:verikora/pull/890).

At 16:49, #889's check is red, for a reason of its own: a formatting line and an old test that encoded the very bug. The train does not block the line for it: it moves on to #890. Team A fixes its red at 17:01, without touching B's files.

At 17:31, the train lands #890. At 17:52, #889 lands on top. The result: 0 conflict with the main branch, 0 rework push, on either one. #890 stayed open 47 minutes, #889 74 minutes.

That is what we wanted to see: the overlap spotted before the second pull request, then both set down one after the other on the same files, with nobody redoing anything.

## S2: the stacked pull request, and the failure

The second scenario was harder. At 16:54, team A opens a second pull request, [#891](repo:verikora/pull/891), for the rest of its ticket. It needs #889's new status, so it stacks it on top. The gate records it: the train will land #891 after its base.

Except that in the meantime, #889 moved. At 17:01, team A pushed the fix of its red to it, and that fix had reformatted a file of the mobile app. #891 had been stacked on the state before.

At 17:54, when #889 landed, the train tried to replay #891 on top. It refused: #891 was not built on the latest version of its base, so it had nothing to replay. #891 found itself in conflict with the main branch, on a file it had never changed. At 18:04, team A had to merge the main branch by hand to clear it. #891 finally landed at 19:05, after one exit from the merge queue that was not its own doing (the mobile check could not unpack the Android development kit it downloads).

I say it as it is: S2 failed. A merge by hand is exactly what the system must avoid. We stopped the run there, without retrying the case until it passed.

## The fix

The cause was precise. The replay of a stacked pull request covered one case only: a stack built on its base's latest version. But the usual case is the other one: the base still takes a push after the stack, because a red is fixed or a review is answered. That case was not covered.

We opened a ticket the same day, with one rule: S2's rerun would be its proof. The fix merged at 20:01 UTC: a stacked pull request is now replayed even when its base moved after it was stacked.

## The S2 rerun

The rerun reproduces the failure exactly, on two new Verikora tickets. Team A opens a base pull request, [#893](repo:verikora/pull/893). A team B stacks its own on it, [#894](repo:verikora/pull/894). Then team A pushes one more commit to its base before it lands: the base moves after the stack, as on 19 November. What we expect: when the base lands, the train replays the stack, and #894 lands with no conflict and no merge by hand.

## What it proves, and what it does not

What it proves: the mechanism, on one case. The gate sees the overlap between two teams before the second pull request exists, and the train lands both on the same files with no conflict and no rework. And when a stack fails, we see it in the log, we know why, and the fix has its own proof.

What it does not prove: a rate. One case bounds nothing. With 0 failures in n cases, the 95 % upper bound of the failure rate is about 3 divided by n: with 2 cases it says nothing useful, it would take 10 to bring it down to 30 %. In S1, the two teams changed different functions of the shared file, so git merged it by itself; the case where two teams change the same lines is not covered here. And both S1 pull requests were fixes, which the gate lets through: a new feature would have been held back, then folded or stacked, and that branch is measured elsewhere.

The measure across all our work is the 14-day study we run again on everything our teams did, due on 3 December. It will say whether overlaps still cost us redone work. Until then, I do not say “our teams no longer step on each other”. I say: on this real case, we saw it coming, and we avoided it.
