On 20 October, I watched our film “The World of Krizaka: l'aube” for the second time, 62 seconds in 4K, made with our birds. My verdict was short: horrible. The voices, the captions, the way the birds interact, all of it was too ugly.

It was not a sloppy film, though. The image and the light were beautiful, I said so myself. It had cost 6.5 hours of an H200 GPU, that is 34.42 $ at the rental company. But a beautiful image does not make a film, and what I had just watched was not a film.

Here is what was wrong, what we changed, what it cost, and what I learned about the difference between improving a prompt and improving a production chain.

Update, October 2026: “l'aube” and “la tournée” are withdrawn. “Le relais” is the brand film. The steps below are the steps of the work, and they serve both.

## Two times I had no loop

The first case is the first batch of clips for Orazaka's showcase. I had given Wan a portrait image cropped to 16:9, and a prompt that described a looping movement. The clips were bad. Each clip costs about 1.20 $ and 18 minutes of H100, so the five I had to redo come to roughly 6 $ and an hour and a half of GPU that produced nothing publishable. The fix holds in two lines: images made at the video's aspect ratio, and a prompt that describes only the movement.

Nobody was looking at the image before launching 18 minutes of GPU. Since then, the generation engine writes to its metrics `imageKept`, the share of the starting image that the frame kept (a portrait cropped to 16:9 keeps a third of it). That does not say whether the result is good, only that it starts with a handicap, but it warns before you pay.

I have a real case for what follows. To test a production chain, I made a short demonstration film of 40 seconds, 10 shots, with [Orazaka](https://dev.orazaka.com), my AI engine. The production file gives the time of each step. At the H100's 3.99 $ an hour, that comes to roughly:

An error found on the keyframe costs one cent. Found in the shot, 36 cents. Found in the edit, 157 times the image. So the loop has to check early, not only at the end.

On this film, 12 shots were filmed for 10 kept. At 0.36 $ a shot, that gives 0.44 $ per kept shot, 1.2 times the price of one run: exactly 1 ÷ p, with p = 10 out of 12. For the film's 35 runs, 5 of them failed, the GPU computation comes to about 7.89 $. I cannot give a cost per film: the pod bill, 23.62 $, mixes several jobs, and I do not put it on one film to get a clean figure.

## What I saw, and heard

I wrote my feedback point by point, because an impression cannot be corrected.

The voice was mechanical and flat, a “GPS effect”: no emotion, no link to the image. The music was far too aggressive. There was no sound design: not one wing beat, not one step, no air. The titles had nothing cinematic about them. The birds moved, but they were not alive, and what they did had nothing to do with the subject. And the story, a disaster, carried nobody.

## The defect was not in the prompt

You might think a better prompt was all it needed. Looking again at how the film had been made, the cause was clear: a short brief went straight to a list of shots. Nobody judged the story before shooting. Each character shot came down to “the bird moves”. The voice had no direction, the music one level, the sound one wind bed.

The defect was in the chain. Here are the steps we added to it. They live in [our AI engine](https://github.com/krizaka/orazaka), because the goal is not to repair one film, but not to make the same mistake on the next.

### 1. A treatment before any shot

Before a shot is drawn, a short brief becomes a story. A theme, a four-beat arc in order (the hook, the journey, the turn, the resolution), the intent of each character taken from its promise, and for each shot a goal, an action, a look, the physics of the body and what happens between two characters. The narrator's lines are added with their direction (emotion, pace, stressed word, pause), musical cues by story beat, and the list of sounds for each shot.

### 2. An automatic critique, twice

The treatment is judged by rules that use no model: is the arc in order, does the turn come after the first third, does every shot have a goal, does every sentence fit its shot (at most 2.6 words per second), does the music start low, peak at the turn and resolve. Then by a model that scores six axes from 1 to 5: transport, arc, characters, life, subject, continuity. The story is shot only if both accept it. Otherwise the reasons go back to the author, twice at most.

For “le relais”, the new film, the treatment had 25 shots for 67 seconds. The rules gave it the top mark, and the model judge gave it 5 out of 5 for transport and 3 out of 5 for life.

### 3. A living, motivated action

This is the point where the birds became actors. The movement of each shot is now written as a small scene: first this, then that, finally that, with the look and its reason, the weight of the body, the effort of a wing beat, a landing, a catch. The phrases “stands there”, “looks around”, “turns its head left then right” are refused by the rule, by name. The shots are shorter, 2.7 to 4 seconds.

Each take is checked afterwards: faithful to the bird's drawing, and alive and on subject (the energy of the movement is measured frame by frame). On the owl scene, where it disputes then recognises the pigeon, the take where it talks moves on 59% of the frames, the one where it laughs on 69%.

Keeping a bird faithful to its drawing took a detour. A free image editor had redrawn a brand bird (the falcon had lost its visor). The keyframe now starts from the rendering of the character itself, placed on an empty set, then retouched at a strength of 0.60 for a character and 0.80 for a group: a group of five was drifting at 0.60.

### 4. A directed voice, chosen blind

We put speech synthesis engines in competition, licence first, with the same synthetic reference voice (never a real person's voice). For each candidate, we measured the movement of the voice's pitch in semitones, the understanding of the words with <Model id="whisper">Whisper</Model>, and the respect of the requested pace.

- <Model id="kokoro">Kokoro</Model>, the voice of the first version: a single delivery, from 8.6 to 11 semitones.
- <Model id="chatterbox">Chatterbox</Model>: very expressive in English (17 to 19 semitones), but it ignores a slow pace, and its French dropped words (an error of 0.67 on one sentence).
- <Model id="zonos">Zonos</Model>: an emotion vector, a rate and a pitch variation per sentence, in English and in French. From 11.8 to 19.4 semitones, every word understood, the requested pace kept. Chosen.
- <Model id="f5-tts">F5-TTS</Model> and <Model id="xtts">XTTS-v2</Model>: refused, their weights are non-commercial.

We also tried having an audio model judge the voice, <Model id="qwen2-audio">Qwen2-Audio</Model>. After calibration, we refused it: it gave about 4 out of 5 to everyone, including the flat voice of the first version, and when asked to choose between two takes it answered “A” 36 times out of 36, in both orders. A judge with a position bias judges nothing. So the voice is measured instead: understanding, pitch movement, pauses, pace. Measurement decides.

We also compared two candidates blind, the voice of the first version and Zonos: each sentence twice, in random order, the answer hidden. The sentences are the birds' promises, such as “The falcon watches: safety first” or “The pigeon delivers, every time”. On the same sentences, the median pitch movement goes from 9.95 semitones for the first voice to 13.6 for the second, and each take of the second is chosen from six at most, first for its accuracy (mean error of 0.033). So it was not an ear that chose, it was the measure.

### 5. Music that follows the story, and fades under the voice

The first version had one piece of music, at the same level from start to finish, too loud. <Model id="ace-step">ACE-Step</Model> now makes one piece per story beat: a dark intro, a warm bed, a rise, a resolution. A mixer sets them at a level that depends on the intensity (from −24 dB for 0 to −9 dB for 1, never full), chains them with fades, and lowers the music under the voice (ducking by the voice, 4 to 1, 60 ms attack, 900 ms release). The final result is brought to −14 LUFS within 0.5, with a peak below −1 dBTP, measured a second time and refused if it falls outside.

On the medina test scene, the measurements give −14.0 LUFS and −1.9 dBTP in English, −14.1 LUFS and −1.3 dBTP in French.

### 6. A sound effect per shot

This is the step where we ran hardest into licences. Every model that makes sound effects from a text or an image had an obstacle: <Model id="mmaudio">MMAudio</Model> is non-commercial, <Model id="stable-audio-open">Stable Audio Open</Model> asks for a licence acceptance that is an account decision, and <Model id="hunyuanvideo">HunyuanVideo</Model>'s excludes the European Union, the United Kingdom and South Korea. So we chose not to use a model. The sound is synthesised from noise, resonators and envelopes: 12 ambiences (rain, storm, wind, lake, waves, a room, dawn, a distant city) and 20 events (wing beats, steps, rustling, paper, an object set down, a clink, bells, bird calls, splashes, a room, a parcel, thunder). A check makes sure no shot is silent and that every character shot has its event.

### 7. Film titles

The titles of the first version did not look like cinema. They are now written with a subtitle engine: the name in spaced capitals whose spacing slowly opens while it stays on screen, and the subtitle in fine serif italic, arriving 0.45 s later. Fade in 1.1 s, fade out 0.8 s. The captions sit higher than before (16% of the height in 16:9, 26% in vertical), with a soft halo instead of a box, two balanced lines of 42 characters at most, never in the top left corner where the viewer's own “AI-generated” badge goes. Everything sits inside the safe zone (10% margin).

### 8. 4K, and what it costs

The shots are filmed at 720p with <Model id="wan22-i2v-a14b">Wan 2.2 I2V A14B</Model> in 40 steps, then upscaled to 4K with <Model id="seedvr2">SeedVR2</Model>. The landscapes come from <Model id="ltx-video">LTX-Video</Model>, much faster. The keyframes are made with <Model id="flux2-klein-4b">FLUX.2 [klein]</Model> on a Mac, at zero dollars of GPU.

For “l'aube”: 23 takes, of which 4 were refused or shortened. The computing time of the runs themselves was 5.5 h (29.00 $); the rest is set-up and waiting. The keyframes, the music and the narration were made on a Mac, at 0 $.

What we measured on an H200 with 141 GB: a Wan 2.2 shot of 81 frames in 720p, 40 steps, takes 1,567 seconds, about 26 minutes, 36 seconds per step. On an H100, it is of the order of 18 minutes per 5-second clip. LTX-Video makes a clip in 11 to 20 seconds. For 4K, SeedVR2 took about 17 minutes per shot with a batch of 5, and 290 seconds with a batch of 21: which makes it possible to run it alongside the generation instead of after it.

For “le relais”, the GPU cost 35.27 $ in total (measured), under a 40 $ ceiling, for 68 seconds of 4K film: 31.12 $ per finished minute, on an H200. About 4.1 $ of that total was lost to set-up, on two servers that the guard stopped. The tests alone (two sessions: the voice, the titles, one full scene) had cost 6.01 $ before the full render. “L'aube” had cost 34.42 $.

### What the GPU taught us about money

A GPU you forget costs as much as a GPU that works. So the machine stops by itself: after 20 minutes without work, or after 6 hours at most. It was this guard that cut “l'aube” at the sixth hour, and the same server had to be picked up once more, for about 30 minutes, for one shot retake and three 4K upscales.

A 200 GB network disk, to keep the models at hand, costs about 14 $ a month doing nothing. We decided to delete it after the last films and to recreate it on demand: redoing the downloads takes a few minutes of GPU, which costs less than keeping it. And on a 94 GB GPU, generation and 4K together ran out of memory: they run one after the other.

“The World of Krizaka: le relais” is 68 seconds in 4K. A parcel, a question: will it arrive? The parcel passes from bird to bird, from city to city, and the music changes genre at each place. ## The line not to cross

These films evoke genres: a rap rhythm, a dinner, a match. We evoke them by original means: light, framing, a piece composed for the occasion (here, for example, a rap track with oud and darbuka). No name or face of a real person, no existing song, no game, no broadcast. The characters are original, the voice starts from a synthetic reference, the sound design uses no model under a restrictive licence. The rules are in the chain's data, and a check verifies them before shooting, not after.

To read a licence without making a mistake, I wrote [this post](/posts/is-that-ai-model-really-open-source/). The licences were read at source on 20 October 2026. They change: check again before relying on them. I am not a lawyer: I read these texts as an engineer.
