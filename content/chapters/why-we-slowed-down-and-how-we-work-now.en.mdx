In [chapter 23](/story/the-trains-second-version-rails-sidings-and-a-repair-train/) I told the story of the rails, the firefighter and the chouf. In [chapter 24](/story/how-we-stay-in-sync/), how we stay in sync. On the morning of 5 November I wrote the CTO a less proud sentence: « I have the feeling we are not moving at the speed we want ». I asked him to study our speed and how we work with the agents, and to give me a plan.

This chapter tells what he found, what we decided the same day, and why. It ends without a result, because the first measure falls on 8 November.

## A feeling, then a measure

The report was made read-only: no issue, no PR, no setting touched. It counts the PRs of 41 repositories since 22 October, the issues, the board and the logs of our tools.

The first line confirmed the feeling. From 22 to 28 October we merged about 40 product features a day. From 29 October to 1 November, 39. From 2 to 5 November, 4.8. That is a drop of 88 %. For Verikora, our priority, we went from 18 features and fixes a day to 4.

The second line surprised me. The pipe itself had not slowed down. An open PR merged in 20 minutes, median. The train took 7 minutes from the head of the queue to the merge. A red PR turned green again in 26 minutes, median too. It was not the CI, not the train, not the review.

The report puts it in one sentence I keep: the work no longer entered the machine.

## Five causes, and none of them was the CI

### Sessions no longer ran

On 1 November, 51 sessions ran. On the 4th, 13. On the 5th, when the report was written, 3. A session only starts when someone launches it, and the rule « relaunch a lane as soon as it finishes » depended on me or on the CTO. And those that started often ended without a PR: 37 % since 31 October.

The case of Verikora, on the evening of the 4th, sums it up. The session built and tested two slices. It could not open their PRs: the repository already had its three PRs, and main stayed red for 886 minutes, because of a smoke test and an automatic dependency update. None of it came from the feature's code. The work stayed on a branch, and up to the report, no Verikora session was launched again.

### The gates had piled up

Since 2 November, every incident had produced its rule. In 72 hours, about ten new gates: the cap cut from five PRs to three, the strongest model and a written lesson after two reds, the firefighter turned into the crack, the ticket's proof in the PR, the acceptance check, the format and the quality analysis at the train's door, freshness, complexity, the list of commit identities. Each one was reasonable. Together, they stacked the stops.

The freeze did the most damage. Of the reds on main from 2 to 5 November, most did not come from product code: 46 automatic dependency updates, 30 quality analyses, 19 releases. Each one froze the whole repository. The watchdog raised 96 « frozen » alerts in four days.

### The factory ate the capacity

I call « the factory » everything that serves to produce: the agents' tools, the rules, the CI, the robots that bump versions. From 2 to 5 November, 60 % of merges came from robots. In 14 days they had opened 748 pin PRs, one per version and per repository. And 52 % of the PRs written by agents went to the factory itself: 83 PRs in four days in the two repositories of rules and tools, roughly one new rule or tool an hour.

The factory had become our agents' first customer. A factory delivers nothing to anyone.

### The board no longer guided

There were 311 cards in « Next », the column of ready work, for a target of about sixty. The platform alone had 202. In 14 days we had created 1,749 issues and closed 950. 94 % were written by agents, often from audits: 179 on 2 November alone.

And the cards were not ready. Only 27 % had the list of files they touch, the one the session requires when it starts. So a session spent its budget redoing the slicing before writing a line.

### Reds we could catch at home

In 14 days, about 2,000 CI runs failed, roughly two reds per branch. A note of 4 November had sorted them: 43 % would have been caught in a few seconds by a local check, 56 % with a common git hook. It was not « too many tests ». It was tests in the wrong place, in CI instead of on the machine, and checks outside the product that blocked the product.

## The meeting on 87 rules

The same day, the CTO brought security, business, UI and UX and the strategy adviser around an inventory: our 87 working rules, each with its date, the place it lives and what it protects. They were reviewed one by one.

The adviser proposed a principle, and everyone took it up. A rule:

- prevents the irreversible;
- acts at the source, in a few seconds;
- names the indicator it must move, or it goes;
- counts its refusals, or it has no right to refuse;
- protects the customer and the founder, not the factory.

With that principle, the sorting was simpler than I thought. Everything about security stayed as it was, unanimously, and security had the last word. The trains remain the only ones that merge, and the release train remains the only gate to production. Honest pages, accessibility, identical texts in French and English: kept too.

Four rules went, among them a local complexity rule that duplicated the quality analysis, and the firefighter, replaced by the crack. Three others, about the board, are now one.

Two conditions came with it. First, every gate logs its refusals: it is not one more rule, it is the measure without which no rule can be judged. Second, the list of what will become strict again is written now: the day a real document can enter our products, the screenshots will block every PR again, and the rest will follow.

And a moratorium. The report proposed fourteen days without a new rule; I chose three, until 8 November. During those three days, a PR that adds a gate, a hook or a workflow step is refused by a check, unless it says why it is an exception. The one factory project allowed was the local check.

To know whether a rule is really in place, the reference does not change: it is the conformance matrix. Every day a script crosses every repository with every rule and answers green, red or out of scope. Not an agent saying « done ». During the moratorium, the matrix's list of rules is frozen at 35, and only the security rules must still reach every repository the same day.

## The firefighter leaves, the crack arrives

In chapter 23, the firefighter woke up when a repository had its PRs at the cap, with a red one among them. The chouf watched for free and woke him at the right time. The firefighter lasted only one day.

What was wrong was where he started. He started from the red PR, and he fixed it. But chapter 23 had already measured it: 92 % of the blocked hours came from red PRs that ended up closed without being merged. The firefighter could put out a fire very well in a house we were about to pull down.

On 4 November I replaced him with the crack, an expert in platforms and software sold online. His first reflex is not the red PR. When he is called, he first understands the goal and the value each open PR brings to the turn we are taking. Then he takes the whole view: the repository's PRs and their cousins in other repositories. What still makes sense stays. A duplicate is closed, with a link to the one we keep. Small PRs that belong together are folded into one. What is out of the direction is closed or parked, with its reason, and its issue goes back to the board. Only then does he fix the red of what is worth landing. And he writes the lesson: why the PRs piled up, at the earliest place it can be caught.

The chouf stays. Every five minutes, without a model, he looks at every repository. If nobody takes a call within a quarter of an hour, a crack session starts by itself, with a cap on turns and duration, and at most four a day.

## What changed on 5 November

That day, 19 PRs changed our tools, in our agents' repository and in the organisation's. The rails are drawn like this now.

A session's path changed too. It has fewer gates, and they sit earlier.

The detail, in the order it matters:

- **Sessions that start by themselves.** When the Verikora or Sovkora lane has a ready card and no live session, a script launches one. The caps are written in the code: at most two at a time, six per 24 hours, 80 turns and 90 minutes each, and a switch to stop it all.
- **A board that guides.** The CTO validates every card that enters Next, with a cap per lane: 15 for Verikora, 15 for Sovkora, 10 for the platform, 10 for security, 0 for Folioviv, which is paused.
- **Audits wait.** What an audit finds goes to a triage column, never straight into Next. Every four days, an incubation lane rereads that column and my ideas: it groups, re-evaluates, promotes what deserves it, and never puts anything into Next itself.
- **A local copy of the board.** Before, every tool reread the board on GitHub at every pass, and my quota of calls was spent. Now a local copy is refreshed every 20 minutes, and every tool that moves a card writes it to the copy too. After the change, I had 4,976 calls left out of 5,000.
- **The local check.** One command runs, on the changed files, what the repository already declares: lint, types, touched tests, generators. It took 4.8 seconds on its own branch, and the common git hook runs it before every push.
- **Every refusal is written down.** One line per refusal, with the tool, the rule, the repository and the PR. That is what will let us judge each rule.

## Three indicators, and a date

The report said it too: we measured a lot, but mostly the factory's activity. With the robots, more than 1,400 merges in 14 days gave a false impression of speed. So we now follow only three figures:

<Steps>
  <Step title="Product features per day">
    The feature or fix PRs merged in the repositories of the turn, without pins, robots or releases.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Hours the product is blocked">
    The sum of the hours a product repository is frozen, or at the cap with a red PR.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Reds that do not come from the product">
    How many reds on main still come from an update, an analysis, a release or a pin.
  </Step>
</Steps>

The first measure is planned for 8 November, at the end of the moratorium. That day, every rule will also pass its review, with its refusals counted. As I write, I have no result. I do not know yet whether the features are coming back, nor how fast. I will say it once I have measured it, even if the figure is bad.

## What I keep

None of our rules was absurd. Each answered a real incident, and I had asked for many of them myself. It was their sum that stopped everything, and nobody looked at it, because no rule said what it cost.

Since 30 October our motto has said: « The flow keeps running, the deadline is ours: it may move, the quality never does. » On 5 November I understood that we had protected quality by stopping the flow. That is not what the motto says. A rule that refuses must prove it protects something, and count what it refuses. Otherwise it is just one more stop on the line.
