When I started Krizaka, I thought I needed a GitHub account and a host. This week I counted. Twelve services, and more than 80 secret values: API keys, tokens, database passwords. For a company where I'm alone, with AI agents writing a large part of the code.

This post is about how you get there, how I keep them, and the mistakes that taught me how. It contains no key, no account id, no path of my folders. That's sort of the point.

## Twelve services, for what

Each service answers a simple need. Code and CI live on GitHub. Packages are published on npm and Maven Central, with a signing key for the latter. The apps and the databases run at DigitalOcean. GPUs are rented by the hour at RunPod. Images and videos are served by Bunny. E-mails go out through Mailgun. The domain names sit with a registrar. Cloudflare provides the anti-robot check on forms. For mobile you need Expo to build the apps, Firebase for notifications and an Apple developer account.


None of them is a luxury. But each brings at least one key, often several: one per environment, one per app, one with fewer rights for CI. That's how twelve accounts become more than 80 secrets.

## Where to keep them

I wrote the basic rule very early: a secret is never in code, never in a repository, never in a chat. Not even in a chat with an AI agent, and especially not there.

In practice, secrets live in a private folder, outside the repositories, on my machine. Deployed apps receive them as secrets of the platform that hosts them, never in clear in a versioned file. The repository's configuration file just holds a placeholder word instead of each value.

To read a secret there's a small script that fetches one key at a time and never prints it: you put it in a variable, you use it, that's all. And when I want to check it's there, I look at its length, not its value.

Environment variables are named by their role, not by the provider. You write `MAIL_PROVIDER=mailgun` rather than a variable named after the vendor:

```bash
MAIL_PROVIDER=mailgun
MAIL_API_KEY=unset
MAIL_DOMAIN=unset
```

Switching providers then means changing a value, not renaming variables across ten repositories.

## The mistakes that taught me

I didn't come up with these rules out of caution. I wrote them after mistakes.

An agent session printed a database's full address in its output, password included. It only wanted to check a configuration. The password had to be changed right away. Since then, a guard runs before every agent command: if the command could print a secrets file, it is refused, with a message explaining how to do it otherwise. Even a home-made "masking" is refused, because that's often where it leaks.

A rented GPU stayed on about two hours after the work was done. Between $7 and $8, for nothing. Its watchdog was supposed to shut it down, but it didn't have the key to do it. I tell the story in [the post about costs](/posts/what-image-and-video-generation-costs/). Since then, a watch loop stops the machine after twenty minutes without work.

A password stayed at its old value somewhere after a rotation. The lesson: a rotation is only done when every place that uses the key has the new one, and you check each place, not just the first.

## What I automate around them

When you're alone, you can't watch everything. So you automate what can be, with agents and CI.

A pull request merges itself when every check is green, and never before. A lock stops two heavy jobs from running at the same time on my machine. A cap limits what AI can spend per day in the development environment. Public forms have an anti-robot check. And every key has as few rights as possible: the one that sends e-mails can only send, it can't even read a list.

The agents write code, but they never see a key. They know it exists, they know its name, they use it through the small script. It's the best way I've found to work fast without being afraid.

## What you can take from it

List your accounts and keys today, even if you're just starting: you'll be surprised by the number. Keep secrets in a separate place, outside your repositories, and read them one key at a time without ever printing them. Name your variables by their role. Give each key as few rights as possible. And when a mistake happens, turn it into a rule right away, then into a tool that prevents it.
