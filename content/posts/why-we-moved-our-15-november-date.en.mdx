We had given ourselves a date: 15 November. It lived in my head, in the plan, in a few conversations. This week, I decided to move it.

It is not a delay we suffered. It is a choice. Rereading what we had measured, I saw that two things were not ready to be shown as they were: the infrastructure, and the command line. Shipping them on time meant shipping them half good. I preferred to drop the date and keep one goal: the best open source architecture we can build, with infrastructure costs crushed now, not later.

This post tells why, what we are going to do, and above all what is not proven yet. Every number that has not been measured is marked "estimate".

## What changed my mind

On 16 October we rendered 25 video clips on a GPU rented by the hour. Five hours in total: 2.8 hours computing, 2.2 hours waiting. A GPU that waits is billed like a GPU that works. That figure is in [decision 078](https://github.com/krizaka/orazaka/blob/main/docs/adr/ADR-078-runpod-serverless-workers-at-zero-idle.md), and it taught me something simple: the computing itself is not the problem. You pay for it once. What you pay for again and again is the waiting and the loading.

The loading, precisely. A 57 GB video model takes 129 seconds to load from the network disk. At every cold start, the user stares at a screen that does not move.

Hence the order that came out of [the infrastructure study](https://github.com/krizaka/orazaka/blob/main/docs/INFRA_PORTABILITY.md): first the waiting, then the loading, then grouping tasks together, and only at the end the serving engine. Changing engine saves nothing while the GPU waits for work.

I had already said it in [the post on portability](/posts/orazaka-from-one-mac-to-anywhere/): cold starts and the cost of a warm GPU were still hard. This time I am tackling them for real.

## Infrastructure: two watchwords

The first: portability, first for running at home. What runs on a Mac, or on a Linux machine with an NVIDIA card, must run without a cloud account. One worker image for Docker, for k3s and for the rented GPU ([orazaka#343](https://github.com/krizaka/orazaka/issues/343)), and an offline bundle of models with signed digests ([orazaka-cli#81](https://github.com/krizaka/orazaka-cli/issues/81)).

The second: cost in the cloud, with the lowest latency we can get. Here is the plan, point by point.

**A GPU that switches itself off.** The serverless GPU goes down to zero when there is nothing to do, and the message queue wakes it when a task arrives. After a task it stays on for about sixty seconds, to absorb a burst of requests with the weights already warm, then it shuts down ([orazaka#342](https://github.com/krizaka/orazaka/issues/342)). This is a trial with a decision gate first, not a decision. The provider's price per second is not confirmed, so my cost-per-clip figures are estimates, and I do not give them here. The goal: several times cheaper per clip than today (estimate).

**Loading faster.** Three levers, described in [worker-media#63](https://github.com/krizaka/orazaka-worker-media/issues/63). Weights in FP8 where the card allows it (Hopper and Ada cards; the others and the Mac keep today's path). Weights copied to a local NVMe disk, then read in parallel with [runai-model-streamer](https://github.com/run-ai/runai-model-streamer) (Apache-2.0). And a manager that keeps the hot models in memory ([worker-media#65](https://github.com/krizaka/orazaka-worker-media/issues/65)). The target: from 129 seconds down to under 12 (estimate, to be measured on a real GPU).

**Computing faster.** [TeaCache](https://github.com/ali-vilab/TeaCache) skips denoising steps when they look alike, and [SageAttention](https://github.com/thu-ml/SageAttention) speeds up attention in 8 bits. Both are Apache-2.0. We are talking about 2 to 2.5 times faster (estimate). But TeaCache was written for the previous version of our model, its last change dates from June 2025, and a speed-up that damages the picture is worth nothing. Nothing goes to production without a quality test that compares with and without.

**Showing what is happening.** A cold start of several tens of seconds becomes a visible step: GPU waking up, weights read (with a real percentage), compute step X of Y, done. The worker publishes these events on the message broker we already have, and they reach the run's event stream, resumed where it stopped if the connection drops ([worker-media#64](https://github.com/krizaka/orazaka-worker-media/issues/64), [orazaka#189](https://github.com/krizaka/orazaka/issues/189)).

**Not answering the same thing twice.** A semantic cache placed just before the model router ([ai-engine#40](https://github.com/krizaka/orazaka-ai-engine/issues/40)), on the PostgreSQL database we already run. It looks for an identical answer first, then a very close one (similarity of at least 0.94). But the safety rules matter more than the cache: it is separated per customer, off by default, and never used for tools, for data that changes, for context that belongs to one person, or for numbers that matter. An invoice, taxes or a lease never go through it.

**Not recomputing the start of every request.** Every call begins with the same long preamble (safety rules, brand context). A server with prefix caching, [SGLang](https://github.com/sgl-project/sglang) or [vLLM](https://github.com/vllm-project/vllm), both Apache-2.0, avoids recomputing it: 60 to 80 percent less GPU time (estimate). [Ollama](https://github.com/ollama/ollama) (MIT) stays as the local fallback when there is no remote GPU ([orazaka#343](https://github.com/krizaka/orazaka/issues/343), [ai-engine#39](https://github.com/krizaka/orazaka-ai-engine/issues/39)). For a provider outage, a fallback chain with a circuit breaker, in the way of [Resilience4j](https://github.com/resilience4j/resilience4j) (Apache-2.0).

All of it sits in a single milestone: [Infra portability](https://github.com/krizaka/orazaka/milestone/4), which runs across four repositories. No date.

## The command line: a session that survives

Here is the scene that made me write the decision. A fairly long agent task: plan, call tools, write code, check. I close my laptop in the middle. Or a worker crashes. Or I reject a draft. Today, I start over, and I pay again for what was already done.

[Decision 086](https://github.com/krizaka/orazaka/blob/main/docs/adr/ADR-086-durable-agent-sessions-on-studio-runs.md) describes how to avoid that. A session is a Studio run of a particular kind. Each step leaves a checkpoint, written once and never changed. You can pause, resume, retry from a given step, or fork a copy. A lease stops two devices from resuming the same session at the same time: the second gets an error and the name of the owner.

On resume, a step already done simply returns the result we recorded: we do not call the model again, so we pay nothing again. A tool that acts outside (sending a message, a payment) writes its intent before acting. If the resume finds an intent with no end, the session stops and asks for approval, instead of doing it again blindly.

The review loop is bounded: a planner, an executor, a critic, and the critic's exact error is sent back to the executor, three tries at most. On the terminal side, `orazaka run` creates the session, and `sessions`, `inspect`, `resume`, `retry --from-step` and `cancel` drive it. Ctrl+C pauses and prints how to resume. And the server never writes to your disk: the command receives the changes and applies them on your side, after you confirm.

Let me be clear about where we stand: this is an accepted decision, not a shipped feature. The first test to pass is easy to say: kill the worker mid-run, resume, and check that the recorded steps are not billed again. Until it passes, I do not say it works.

## Where we departed from the first idea

This is the part I like writing least and that matters most.

No graph library at first. The starting idea went through an agent graph, and the obvious library for that is [langgraph4j](https://github.com/langgraph4j/langgraph4j) (MIT). It is alive and fits our version of Spring AI, but it has a single maintainer, its own serialisation and its own tables. It would have created a second source of truth for the state of a run, outside our audit. So we extend what Studio already does. Langgraph4j stays a possible trial later, if we ever need branches beyond the review loop. I also set aside the workflow engines: Temporal adds a system to operate, Restate has a BSL licence, and Inngest an SSPL one, not the one the study believed. The [ACP](https://github.com/agentclientprotocol/agent-client-protocol) protocol (Apache-2.0), which lets editors talk to an agent, will come in a later phase, on top of the same interface.

No model names before they run. The starting study talked about roles with fine names and models of 32 billion parameters. None of them exists in our code or our catalogue. What really runs is <Model id="qwen25-7b">Qwen2.5 7B</Model> and its coding version. The loop will be proven with those, with run ids, and the bigger models will be named once they have run.

No Modal and no "per prediction" service as the platform: the first ties us to its tool, the second makes us lose control of the weights and the licences. Modal stays a point of comparison ([orazaka#324](https://github.com/krizaka/orazaka/issues/324)). And no Redis for the cache: it is not deployed, and PostgreSQL already does the job.

## What you can take from it

When a date forces you to ship something you are not proud of, look at what it protects. For me, it protected nothing. It pushed me to optimise the engine while the GPU was waiting, and to write a command that forgets everything at the first crash.

Measure first where the time and the money go. Write "estimate" next to everything you have not measured, even when it is awkward. And keep one plan, in the open, that anyone can read in the repository.
