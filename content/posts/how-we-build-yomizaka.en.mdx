A few weeks ago I was in a café with a friend who manages apartment buildings in Montréal. He took out his phone, turned the screen toward me and showed me a pay stub, a PDF. An applicant for a two-bedroom had sent it the day before.

"Do you see anything?"

I didn't. The logo was clean, the amounts added up, the deductions looked like Québec ones. He didn't see anything either, and that was exactly what bothered him. He gets dozens of these a month. He looks at them by eye, forwards them by e-mail to the owner with the ID and the rest, and hopes.

Then he scrolled. The next pay stub was not even a PDF: it was a photo, taken at an angle on a kitchen table, one corner folded.

It is not a matter of bad will. It is a matter of tools. Nobody ever gave him anything but his eyes and an inbox. That is exactly where Yomizaka wants to go.

## What Yomizaka already does

The idea fits in one sentence: read before you sign. You drop a lease, a contract or an invoice, and Yomizaka reads it against the rules in force. For every finding, it shows you the exact line of the document it relies on.

Take a real test lease. When it arrives, Yomizaka first recognises what it is: a residential lease, with its dates, its rent, its deposit and its language. It also spots the personal information inside and offers you a redacted version. The document is kept in Canada, encrypted, until you delete it.

Then comes the check. The text is read rule by rule against the Civil Code of Québec and the regulation on the mandatory lease form, as they apply on a given date. On that lease, on 17 October, three blocking issues came out of nine rules: a security deposit, post-dated cheques and a clause that forbids children. The Civil Code forbids all three, and each came back with its article and the exact sentence of the lease.

I keep the yellow box on purpose. The Québec corpus has not been reviewed by a lawyer yet, and the screen says so. Until it is, this is a sorting aid, not legal advice.

And the run id is the trace. Yomizaka never calls a model itself: Orazaka, our engine, does the analysis, and every analysis keeps what ran, with which version, on which rules. If someone questions a finding in six months, we can find exactly where it came from.

There is also the examination of the file itself, the one that would have helped my friend in the café. Yomizaka looks at which software produced the PDF, on which dates, how many times it was saved again, which fonts it uses. Out of that come signals on three levels: observed, unusual, inconsistent. Never a "fake" stamp. A PDF that went through an editor is not necessarily a forgery, and I don't want a tool deciding in a person's place.

## How a rule is written

The least visible part is the most important one. A rule is never written from memory. We start from the text of the law, we cite the article, and if we cannot read the source, the rule waits.

In practice, a rule is data, not code. Here is the deposit rule as it sits in Orazaka's pack. I shortened the pattern: the real one fills a whole line, because it recognises "security deposit", "one month's rent as a guarantee" or a percentage, and ignores "no deposit".

```json
{
  "ruleId": "QC-LEASE-004",
  "title": "Dépôt de garantie interdit — seul le premier mois peut être exigé d'avance",
  "severity": "BLOCKING",
  "method": "DETERMINISTIC",
  "citation": "C.c.Q., art. 1904, al. 1",
  "check": {
    "type": "FORBIDDEN_PATTERN",
    "pattern": "(?i)(?<!aucun )(?<!sans )d[ée]p[ôo]ts?\\s+(?:de\\s+)?(?:garantie|s[ée]curit[ée])…"
  },
  "onPass": "Aucun dépôt de garantie n'est exigé."
}
```

`DETERMINISTIC` means no model judges this rule: a pattern finds it or does not, and the result can be reproduced. The model reads and summarises; it does not invent the law. Every rule comes with two examples written from the requirement: a lease that meets it, a lease that does not. Then a real run on a test lease is the acceptance test. That run, on 17 October, is the one that caught the deposit.

The whole ruleset carries its date, its revision and its review status:

```json
{ "rulesetId": "qc-residential-lease", "version": "2024-02-21", "revision": 2, "reviewStatus": "TO_REVIEW_BY_LAWYER" }
```

That `reviewStatus` is what shows the yellow box. The day a lawyer has read it, the data changes, and so does the screen.

It is slower than asking a model to "read the lease and say what is wrong". But an answer that does not cite its source is not one I can defend in front of someone about to sign a twelve-month lease.

## What we are working on right now

Since the café, we have been moving on several fronts at once. They arrive bit by bit, and I would rather show them one at a time, when they really run.

**Photos.** The pay stub shot at an angle on the kitchen table is the normal case, not the exception. We are wiring up the reading of photos and scans: when a page has no readable text, Orazaka reads the image with a vision model, then the same check applies, with the same rules and the same trace. A photo of an ID follows the same path, with a person deciding as soon as something is unclear.

**Folders.** A property manager does not think in documents; they think in buildings and applicants. Yomizaka's library is already organised in libraries, collections and folders: a building, a unit, an applicant. We are now building the applicant's file end to end. The applicant receives a unique link, drops their photos and PDFs, reads and signs their consent, and nothing goes through e-mail. The documents are examined, then purged under a clear rule, and the owner receives a link to a report, not the documents. The report shows the effort ratio, that is the rent divided by the verified net income, as a fact with its source. Not as a score. The decision stays with the person renting.

**Data, with an action at the end.** A lease holds dates, and those dates make deadlines. For a one-year lease, the rent-increase notice must go out between six and three months before the end (article 1942 of the Civil Code). With a few hundred units, it is easy to forget one. We pull those dates out of every lease, and each deadline becomes a line with a button: prepare the notice, send it, done. It is the same idea as in Akinaza, where the shop owner gets three figures and a button every Monday.

**Your Yomizaka.** When you arrive, Yomizaka asks who you are reading for: tenant, landlord, freelancer or business. Your answer picks the check offered first and the words on screen, never the rules. Language, light or dark theme, profile picture and preferences are set in one place, and they follow your account from one device to another.

**The web, the phone, the Mac.** Yomizaka runs on the web, designed for the phone first. The native apps are coming out now: a first test build for Android was published today, iOS follows from the same code, and a Mac app is on its way for those who manage their files from the office. All three talk to the same API, with the same account.

There are also things we will not do until a lawyer tells us they are allowed. Criminal background checks for housing are one of them. They stay off.

## The order

I can't say in how many weeks all this will be ready. I do know the order: what protects people first, what saves time next. Photos and the applicant's file first, because that is where forgeries and leaks are. Deadlines next. Each piece ships when it runs on real documents, with its trace.
